# Nassir "Leight" Okoro

A solo Ironsworn: Starforged campaign.

Courier.  Shadow-worker.  Former Cartel operative.  Now free, broke, and alone in a beat-up freighter with one tank of gas and a gift for making things disappear.

## What Is This Page?

This is a bit of an experiment using the "digital garden" technique to build a sci-fi setting and write a story within it.  Rather than just sitting down and writing a novel, you grow the world and its stories organically.  You effectively create the in-story version of Wikipedia.  You create pages for various people, locations, events, etc in the universe you are creating and, by linking them together, you start building out relationships and interactions.  The stories emerge from those naturally.

## Where Do I Even Start?

You have a few options.  If you just want to read a sci-fi story, the [[Journals/Journals|Journals]] are effectively chapters of the "novel" which currently tells the story of Leight in this setting.  You could start there for a traditional "I read the story" approach.

Alternatively, the [[Truths]] are the fundamental baseline truths of the setting.  Does magic exist?  Are there aliens?  What's the tech level?  Things like that.  These are the foundations of the setting and you may wish to know more about the setting before diving in.  Some could be considered storyline spoilers since characters generally don't know all of the inner workings of the universe they live in.  Some of these Truths are going to be more prevalent than others, some may become important later, some may never make any tangible difference in our story.  Who knows.

Another option is to explore it like you would explore Wikipedia.  Pick a page that looks interesting and then go link-surfing from there.  This is almost certainly going to contain a lot of spoilers.  There are tons of details in the various pages that have not made it into the story, and may never surface; lots of threads that may or may not ever get pulled.

I have some links below to some pages I think work as good entry points into the story.

## Why Would You Do This To Yourself?

I find I enjoy world-building and thinking ideas through, and that the fiction starts to emerge as you explore ideas and their ramifications.  This is easier and more enjoyable for me than just "sit down and come up with a story".  But honestly, the world-building is the point and the more enjoyable part for me, so why not lean into that?

One nice thing about this format is that I am not locked into one character's story.  If I come up with a new character and want to explore them more, I just add pages off of that character's node.  Side characters can effectively get their own entire episodes/novel/series this way.  If you read and find a character you like, and assuming I do this right, you can go to that character's page and see every chapter/story/page they were referenced in, making it easier to write and expand those side stories.  I may decide after a few interactions with Basil that this character is actually more interesting and I want to learn more about them.  No problem.  This isn't Leight's story, it's the story of this setting: The Forge.

Another nice thing is getting to explore all the tangents which, in turn, generate new plot hooks and ideas.  For example: when I decided that the residents of Celadon lived in repurposed shipping containers, I decided that I needed to know how big that made their buildings and what that meant for living conditions and how it applied to oversized structures like the mechanic repair bay.  But why would a spacefaring civilization's "standard shipping container" be the same as ours?  They would have entirely different design constraints.  By the time I was finished pulling that thread I had invented an entire industrial standard with a history, commercial rivalries, genericized trademarks, and a modular system that scales from a single room to an entire space station.  None of that will likely ever appear in the prose directly, but it informs every scene set in a building or a space station, which in turn affects ship design and vehicle design, etc.  The world feels more solid because I know what the walls are made of (literally), even if the characters never comment on it.

If that idea didn't absolutely terrify you, you may appreciate the [Worldbuilding for Masochists](https://worldbuildingformasochists.podbean.com/) podcast.

## Where Is This Coming From?

As I said above, this is a bit of an experiment.  At its heart this is a game of [Ironsworn: Starforged](https://tomkinpress.com/pages/ironsworn-starforged), a DM-less story-driven sci-fi tabletop RPG.  Layered on top and woven into that I am using [Bucket of Bolts](https://mouseholepress.itch.io/bucket-of-bolts) by Jack Harrison to design the ships and their histories.  For exploring new settlements and planets I am using [i'm sorry did you say street magic](https://seaexcursion.itch.io/street-magic) by Caro Asercion and [Microscope](https://lamemage.itch.io/microscope) by Ben Robbins.  When I want a little inspiration or flavor I'm looking at various other flavor-generating guide books such as [Remarkable Cults and Their Followers](https://loresmyth.com/en-ca/products/remarkablecults) by Loresmyth Games.  I have a copy of [Sundered Isles](https://tomkinpress.com/pages/sundered-isles) - Starforged for pirates - in my back pocket just in case that comes in handy.  I may look for a similar ruins/dungeon generation tool if/when the story calls for one, though I don't have one in mind yet.

**What does any of that mean?  How can you play six games at once?**

Starforged is the sci-fi variation of a game called Ironsworn.  It is designed to be played either solo or as a cooperative group without one person coming up with the story like many similar games.  Instead of asking the DM to set the scene and come up with plot lines, you tell your story.  When you need to make a decision like "do I get caught picking the guy's pocket?" you roll some dice and "consult the oracle" which is really just a bunch of tables with various outcomes.  The story dictates when and how often you do it.  If you have a story in mind and just want to write, you do that.  If you get to a point where you think maybe you are being too easy on your character(s), you can spice things up a little.  If you hit a spot where you need a prompt, you consult the oracle.  The point of this style of game system is to advance the narrative, not to have multiple books of rules about what you can and can't do.

**OK, I can see that being interesting, but what about the rest of those games?**

They aren't mutually exclusive!  Since each of them is rules-light and focuses on telling a story instead of dictating a setting or a rules system, you can mix and match pretty nicely.  Admittedly, some of them are intended for fantasy settings, so I'm using those as prompts and ideas that I can port into my setting rather than using them straight up, but the point stands.

Bucket of Bolts is an interesting "game" where you start with a ship brand new off the factory floor, then give it life by exploring the owners and the changes and adventures they had in it.  The game is intended to end with the ship being decommissioned but I used it to come up with three previous owners of The Hulking Anguish and how those stories carried over into what the ship is today.

Street Magic (DYSSM) and Microscope are both world-building games with different approaches.  DYSSM is intended to be a game where the group walks around a new town and describes what they see and notice, thereby building the settlement collaboratively.  Microscope (ironically) takes a wider approach and is more useful for planet-scale ideas.  You come up with the general timeline and the major historical events and figures of that world.  The result is location pages like Celadon that feel lived-in because they were built piece by piece, starting from initial settlement and advancing to "today," rather than invented all at once.

The Loresmyth books are just fun.  They focus on fantasy settings but have some fun ideas for plot hooks and settings.  Not really a game, just some more writing prompts and ideas.

## How Often Does It Update?

That's a loaded question.  I am trying to add a little each night, but we all know how that goes.  It may just be a sprinkle of flavor on an existing page, a new narrative/prose Journal entry, or just creating a skeleton for a new character or location we haven't met yet, but I want to try to expand this little universe a bit each day and see where it goes.
