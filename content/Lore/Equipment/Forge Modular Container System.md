# Forge Modular Container System

**Type:** Universal structural/logistics standard
**Manufacturer (original):** Pearson Industrial (structural frames), Corvin (cladding)
**Common Use:** Shipping, station architecture, habitation, gantry scaffolding, equipment mounting

## Overview

The universal building block of the Forge.  Two separate products, two separate manufacturers, used together so universally that people forget they're distinct: the Pearson Industrial Platform (the structural frame) and the Corvin HexClad panelling (the skin).  Together they form everything from shipping containers to space station modules to worker housing to crane gantries.

## The PIP (Pearson Industrial Platform)

The base unit.  A cubical structural frame: corner posts, edge beams, floor plate, roof plate.  Standardized connection points on all faces.  Made from a lightweight nonferrous polyalloy optimized for mass (iron is rare and culturally significant in the Forge; everyday construction doesn't use it).

A PIP can be used bare or clad.  Bare PIPs serve as gantry scaffolding, antenna mounts, equipment platforms, raised walkways, shelving structures, and anything else requiring a rigid modular framework.  Clad PIPs become enclosed volumes: cargo containers, habitation modules, pressure vessels.

Where PIPs join, a few millimeters of raised frame rail remain on the interior floor.  Trivial in most applications; enough to trip a sleepy resident or mildly annoy a hard-wheeled cart.

The name "PIP" is stamped on every frame.  Nobody alive expands the acronym consistently.  In casual speech: a "pip."

## Corvin HexClad Panelling

The panels.  Bolted to the exterior faces of a PIP frame.  Stamped with a hexagonal relief pattern that serves triple duty:

- **Structural:** Stiffens the panel against bending without adding material weight.
- **Logistical:** The hex relief provides anti-slip nesting when panels are stacked for transport.  A stack behaves as a unit instead of a deck of cards.  Trade-off: stacked panels can't collapse truly flat (dead space between them).
- **Brand identity:** The stamp pattern *is* the trademark.  Visible from across a room regardless of sealant, oxidation, or bio-fouling.

The original Corvin® HexClad™ pattern is the most complex and detailed.  The market is saturated with knockoffs that kept the hex motif but simplified it at lower price points.  All are functionally interchangeable and interlock when stacked; except one particular cheap brand that doesn't interlock properly and is universally hated by anyone who works with their hands.  It still gets ordered when bureaucrats look only at the price tag.

### Terminology

- **Full marketing:** Corvin® HexClad™ Polyalloy Panelling System
- **Industry/mechanic:** "hex clad," "hex panel"
- **Everyone else:** "corvin" or just "panel"

## Sealant Coating

Once panels are assembled on a frame, the entire exterior is coated with a spray-on elastomeric sealant.  Approximately 5mm thick.  Texture and consistency similar to truck bed liner: slightly rubberized, textured for grip.

Functions:

- **Pressure membrane:** Bridges bolt heads and panel seams; holds atmosphere against vacuum for space transport and station use.  The structural load is borne by the PIP frame; the sealant just maintains the seal.
- **Handling grip:** The textured surface helps automated equipment and docking arms manipulate units.
- **Corrosion protection:** Isolates dissimilar metals at fastener points, preventing galvanic corrosion.

Degradation (predictable, visible):

1. **Fresh:** Smooth, uniform color.  Hex pattern visible but muted beneath the coating.  Manufacturer's logo legible.
2. **Grip loss:** The rubbery texture wears smooth first.  Surface becomes slick.
3. **Cracking:** Stress points (edges, bolt heads, seams, corners) crack from thermal cycling and UV exposure.
4. **Flaking:** Patches delaminate.  Hex pattern re-emerges.  Bare alloy begins oxidizing.

In space, the sealant requires recoating every few years.  Planetside, nobody bothers; it just slowly sheds.

## Alloy Degradation (without sealant)

The polyalloy oxidizes distinctively:

- **Mid-life:** Surface dulls.  Fine bluish dust forms, collecting in hex channels, seams, and anywhere water pools and evaporates.  Touch an old panel and your hand comes away blue-grey.
- **Old:** The oxidation layer deepens.  Water carves pale streaks through it (bare metal exposed by runoff, re-oxidizing, repeating; tide-mark patterns).  Biological fouling (green-black mold) layers on top in humid environments.
- **Failing:** Stress cracks propagate from fastener points and corners.  Metal thins where water has concentrated.  Tone changes when struck (a diagnostic tap with a wrench is how long-timers check structural integrity).  A fatigue crack propagates invisibly until the day the panel gives.

The bluish dust is distinctive and ubiquitous in older settlements.  Invisible to residents; immediately noticeable to visitors.  Tracked on boot soles, smudged on doorframes, collected in gutters.

## Dimensions

A pip is a 12-foot cube (~3.7m per edge).  All other configurations scale from this:

- **Pip:** 12×12×12 ft (144 sq ft floor, 12 ft ceiling)
- **Pea:** 24×12×12 ft (288 sq ft floor)
- **Slab:** 24×24×12 ft (576 sq ft total footprint; as hab: 432 sq ft interior + 144 sq ft balcony)
- **Pod:** 24×24×24 ft (576 sq ft per floor × 2 floors = 1,152 sq ft; or single volume with 24 ft ceiling)

The 12 ft ceiling gives generous vertical space; enough to build a loft or mezzanine inside a single-pip-height room.

**Note:** A larger unit (3 pods arranged as a 4×4×4 pip cube, called a "mod" / module) likely exists as the standard module in space station architecture.  Rings are constructed from mods.  TBD; dimensions and implications to be addressed when Paxton's ring structure is fully defined.

Scale language: pip (room) → pod (house) → mod (building/city block).

- **Engineers/specs:** PIPs and pods.  Formal designations.  Supply manifests.
- **Dock workers:** Peas and slabs.  The things they move daily.
- **Residents:** "My slab," "a two-slab unit."
- **Bureaucrats:** "Pearson/Corvin Modular Habitat" (on inspection forms nobody reads).

## Design Notes

### Pressurized vs. Cargo Use

A clad, sealed unit is vacuum-rated and can be mounted on external gantries, ship hulls, and station frames exposed to space.  The same unit planetside no longer needs the seal; the sealant degrades without maintenance and nobody cares because atmosphere is free.

### Modularity

Every panel is bolted (not welded) to the frame.  Any face can be wall, door, window, mesh, or absent.  Configuration is a field decision:

- Removing a corner's panels creates an L-shaped interior with a roofed outdoor porch (common in habitation).
- Removing shared panels between adjacent pips creates larger interior volumes.
- Removing ceiling/floor plates between stacked units creates double-height spaces.
- Leaving a frame bare (no panels) creates open scaffolding.

### Mixed-Use Construction

Larger structures (workshops, hangars, mess halls) often use PIPs as structural building blocks rather than living spaces.  Stack pips as thick walls with exterior panels and interior faces left bare (creating cubby storage in the open frames).  Span a roof across the tops.  The interior is column-free open space; the PIP walls carry all structural load.

### Material Grades

Corvin-grade polyalloy (the container standard) is the lightweight/cheap option.  Heavier hull-grade alloys exist for ships, stations, and military hardware.  Specialty alloys serve engines, weapons, and medical equipment.  In field-modified structures, mixed materials are common: hull plate in stress areas, corvin elsewhere.  The material differences are visible (different aging patterns, different weight, different surface finish).

## Cultural Significance

Iron's rarity and cultural weight in the Forge exists precisely because everyday life is surrounded by lightweight polyalloy composites.  A person might go their entire life touching nothing but space-age alloys and composites.  Swearing on black iron means something because nobody *builds* with the stuff.
