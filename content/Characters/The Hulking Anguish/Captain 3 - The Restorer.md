## Card 1: Personality / Who was the captain?
### Clubs = labor, craft, ingenuity.  Queen = dramatic/face card.

### Prompt
A master craftsperson.  The Queen of Clubs is someone whose identity IS their work.  Not a dilettante or a hobbyist; this is someone with real skill, real experience, and strong opinions about how things should be done.  An old soldier who defines themselves by what they can build and fix with their hands.  They bought this ship the way a retired mechanic buys a rusted-out classic: it's a project, a purpose, a reason to get up in the morning.

### Interpretation
This owner was definitely a retired combat engineer and/or loadmaster "coming home".  They served on the cargo variant of this ship and those were the best (and worst) years of their life.  Restoring "the old gal" was an act of near reverence and faith; repaying the debt for the one that saved his bacon so many times.  This is the B-52 of the Forge; he served on one, his father served on one, HIS father probably served on one.

## Card 2: Mark left behind (modification/addition)
### Spades = hard, martial, dangerous.  10 = dramatic.

### Prompt
The one spade in a hand full of clubs.  The restorer's work was mostly about craft and labor, but this card says one modification was specifically martial or dangerous in nature.  Something hard-edged.  Maybe they brought the ship's military bones back to the surface: reinforced something, added something defensive, restored a weapons system, or hardened the hull in a way that goes beyond "civilian freighter."  This is the old soldier showing through the gardener.

### Interpretation
This person fixed and flew on this exact model for years in combat and knows exactly what its weakest points are.  They adamantly reinforced that strut that always wore prematurely, they upgraded that compressor that always died.  They didn't replace the cockpit patch job, though.  It is still there as tribute to the pilot who couldn't have possibly survived that blast, even if captain 3 doesn't know their story, it's clearly a kill-shot.  The same with the over-pressurized cabin.  To his experienced eyes, it was clearly done as an accommodation for a previous pilot and some things you don't "fix".

## Card 3: Event that happened aboard
### Clubs = labor, craft, ingenuity.  2 = subtle/minor.

### Prompt
Something small and quiet happened during this era.  Not dramatic, not violent.  A moment of craft or ingenuity that mattered in a small way.  Maybe a problem solved cleverly.  Maybe a quiet breakthrough in the restoration.  Maybe the moment they realized the project would never be finished, and made peace with it.  The 2 says this is understated; it's the kind of thing only the ship would remember.

### Interpretation
The garden is the moment of silence and reflection.  Turning that extravagant floral waste of space into a sustainable food supply and leaving it "for now" about his military restoration project was an act of meditation and self-reflection now lost to time.

## Card 4: Secret or residue left behind
### Clubs = labor, craft, ingenuity.  5 = moderate-low.

### Prompt
Something crafted, left behind.  Modest in scope.  Not a grand secret or a valuable treasure; a small piece of workmanship that outlasted its maker's time with the ship.  A hand-finished detail.  A tool left in a compartment.  A repair done with such care that it's better than the original.  Something that says "someone who loved this work was here."

### Interpretation
I love the "box of parts".  It's another classic cliche "Craigslist project car" trope.  They ALWAYS come with a box of random parts.  Maybe these ones are better cleaned and ready to install, but it's still the classic box-o-parts the previous owner threw in for "free" with the car.  And, much like the box, they aren't well documented or organized so maybe the protagonist doesn't even know exactly what he has in there yet, but a mechanic will recognize the utility.  There are also a few hand-carved wooden switches and dials.  He could have used factory parts from the scrap yard like everything else, but these particular ones have Meaning.

# Narrative

[[Oren Herschel]] bought the *Miravel's Reverie* at its second auction and immediately hated the name.  He knew this airframe.  The SH-56 "Lurch" had been flying since before his grandfather's time: the B-52 of the Forge, ugly and immortal and everywhere.  Oren's grandfather had served on one.  His father had served on one.  Oren himself had spent the best and worst years of his life as a combat engineer on the SH-56C cargo variant, keeping them flying in conditions that should have killed everyone aboard.  Buying this particular Lurch, even dressed up in civilian clothes, was coming home.

He restored the military designation: *Hulking Anguish*.  "That's not her *name*," he probably said to nobody in particular, stripping the gilt lettering off the hull.

The restoration was an act of love.  Oren stripped the luxury fittings, rebuilt the cargo rail system (got two-thirds of the way down the hold), and reinforced the structural weak points he'd seen fail on a dozen other Lurches in his career.  He wasn't using junkyard scrap; he was machining parts by hand, restoring the ship to a specific iteration of the airframe.  The one his family flew.

He left certain things alone.  The cockpit breach-patch: clearly a kill-shot, and you don't sand over a grave.  The over-pressurized flight deck: one old soldier recognizing another's accommodation without needing to understand why.  Some PCMS cameras he found and disabled, though he missed others buried deeper in the wiring.

And the greenwall.  He'd been stripping out all of Mira's luxury touches, and then he got to the greenwall and stopped.  Stood there looking at it.  Started planting vegetables instead of pulling it out.  That was the moment the project stopped being about erasing previous owners and started being about living with the ship.

The hand-carved wooden knobs on three cockpit switches weren't a cosmetic choice.  Oren's grandfather had turned a set on his lathe during a long deployment, and every Herschel Lurch since then carried wooden knobs on those specific switches.  Tradition.  Family.  The fourth switch was still bare polymer when Oren passed.

He died before the work was done.  Natural causes; he simply ran out of time.  His heir, a grandson carrying on the family tradition aboard an SH-56 somewhere in the black, was off-world in military service when Oren passed.  The property was prime real estate.  Oren had bought that plot before it was worth anything, and someone with money and lawyers decided the view was wasted on an old man's hangar.  The "no next of kin" paperwork closed faster than military mail could reach the grandson.  By the time he heard, the land was sold, the ship was gone, and the documentation was clean enough to make contesting it pointless.

To the new property owner, the ship was just the half-disassembled project in the barn that came with the real estate.  They put it up for resale without knowing or caring what it was.

Somewhere aboard, Oren left a build log.  He was the kind of man who documented everything: what he'd done, what he planned to do, what parts he'd made, what he'd left alone and why.  Nobody's found it yet.  But it's there, meticulous and patient, waiting for someone to care enough to look.

The grandson is out there.  Quiet, competent, patient.  Following registration transfers.  He'd recognize the wooden knobs.

Our protagonist bought it cheap.  Who wants a half-restored Frankenstein ship?
